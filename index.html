Problems for Language Model Introspection

Juan Cadile, University of Rochester, Department of Philosophy · jcadile@ur.rochester.edu · juancadile.com
Nick Meyer, Columbia University, Department of Computer Science · nick.meyer@columbia.edu · nickmeyer.xyz

Claims of LM introspection concern distinct capacities with different evidential demands. Current evidence meets some first-order criteria, but none of the studies we review meets them together or shows metacognitive representation.

Motivation

Language models (LMs) produce reports that describe their own internal states. Whether these reports surface from a process we’d be inclined to call introspection is less clear—and contested.

Part of the problem is conceptual imprecision: different papers use the same term for different capacities, or different terms for the same capacity. Consequently, results presented as supporting or challenging “introspection” often bear on different claims.

Here, we attempt to make this more clear by: 1) defining introspection for language models, 2) operationalizing its study into one framework, and 3) evaluating existing empirical claims under this framework.

Four routes to a self-report

Ascribing introspection: capacities and criteria

[1]Lindsey, J. (2025). Emergent Introspective Awareness in Large Language Models. transformer-circuits.pubFollowing Lindsey [1], we take introspection in its robust sense to be producing a report about an internal state that is accurate, grounded in it, derived from internal activations, and mediated by a metacognitive representation of it. Each row is one of seven capacities that studies in the literature have associated with, or labeled as, “introspection.” The first six are ordered from coarse to fine: detecting that something occurred (and whether this depends on what it is), locating it, identifying its content and its source, and characterizing it relative to other states. The seventh, control, is not a further level of grain but a use of the others: control counts as introspective only when a reading of the current state guides the change. Evidence for a given capacity supports an ascription of introspection for that capacity only. Tests of a finer capacity often presuppose a coarser one (asking where a perturbation is tells the model that there is one). So evidence for a coarser capacity does not establish a finer one, and evidence for a finer capacity cannot establish a coarser one.

The columns ask what evidence each capacity must meet before it supports an ascription of introspective ability. For these, we adapt four evidential criteria from Lindsey [1]: accuracy, grounding, internality, and metacognitive representation. Each criterion excludes a different alternative explanation. Accuracy excludes guessing; grounding excludes reports that would come out the same whatever the internal state; internality excludes reading the model’s own output; metacognitive representation excludes a mere first-order detector. Results that meet the first three criteria together, for the same reports, can establish a first-order route from internal state to report. Only results that also meet the fourth can distinguish a metacognitive route from a first-order one.

[18]Comșa, I. M., & Shanahan, M. (2025). Does It Make Sense to Speak of Introspection in Large Language Models? arXiv:2506.05068Views differ on how many of these criteria introspection needs. On interpretationist views, accuracy and grounding are enough: Comșa & Shanahan [18] count any accurate report causally linked to the state, including one the model infers from its own output. Because the table scores each criterion separately, it can be read under any of these views. Together, the four are plausibly sufficient, unless introspection also requires acquaintance with phenomenal states, as some views hold. Even then, they are jointly sufficient only when all hold on the same reports, for the same task. Satisfying the criteria is not compositional—it is not enough for studies run under separate experimental regimes to each meet different criteria and “sum to introspection.” Studies can combine only when they test the same reports under matched conditions.

Our empirical work

[10]Hahami, E., Sinha, I., & Jain, L. (2026) (b). Introspection Fine-Tuning (IFT): Training Small LLMs to Introspect. arXiv:2607.14111[11]Singh, S., Linzen, T., & Ravfogel, S. (2026). Can LLMs Introspect? A Reality Check. arXiv:2605.26242A case study on Llama-3.2-1B: 45 adapters trained to say which of ten sentences carried an injected concept vector, with Introspection Fine-Tuning (Hahami et al. [10]) and our own method, DC-SFT, at three strengths of a KL anchor and five seeds, plus control adapters trained on random vectors, shuffled labels or no injection. The fine-tuned case is the one Singh et al. [11] set aside.

Two main results

  1. Localization · grounding

    The injected state causes the report, but a note alone also sets it off.

    Patching out the injected state leaves only 8.7% and 13.5% of reports (pre-registered; Fig. 2). Yet with nothing injected, a note naming a sentence makes 42 of 45 adapters report an injection there (Fig. 3). Untrained models read the note’s meaning and ignore a denial (1–2%, Llama 1B and 8B); the trained adapters follow the number either way (100%), on two new sentence draws too. The cause is real; the report is not grounded.

  2. Content-sensitive detection · grounding

    Concept vectors beat random ones, but not because of the concept.

    At 1σ, a concept vector is noticed on 31–44% of trials and a random direction on 10–11% (Fig. 5). The mean of the other concepts’ vectors is noticed on every trial, and adapters trained on random vectors show no concept edge. The edge comes from a direction all concept vectors share.

Together: training produces a readout that passes accuracy and internality, and fails grounding.
